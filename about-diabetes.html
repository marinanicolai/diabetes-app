About Diabetes

Diabetes is a complex, chronic illness requiring continuous medical care with multifactorial risk reduction strategies. Learn the facts, symptoms, and what you can do.

What Is Diabetes?

Diabetes is a disease that occurs when your blood glucose, also called blood sugar, is too high. Blood glucose is your main source of energy and comes from the food you eat.

Insulin, a hormone made by the pancreas, helps glucose from food get into your cells to be used for energy. Sometimes your body doesn't make enough — or any — insulin or doesn't use insulin well.

Glucose then stays in your blood and doesn't reach your cells. Over time, having too much glucose in your blood can cause health problems.

Key Facts

  • 🩸   Over 38 million Americans have diabetes
  • ⚠️   1 in 5 people with diabetes don't know they have it
  • 💰   Diabetes costs the US $412 billion annually
  • 💊   Diabetes is the 8th leading cause of death in the US

Types of Diabetes

There are several types of diabetes. The most common are type 1, type 2, and gestational diabetes.

💉

Type 1 Diabetes

An autoimmune disease where the body doesn't produce insulin. Requires insulin therapy to survive. Accounts for ~5–10% of all diabetes cases.

Learn About Type 1 →
🩺

Type 2 Diabetes

The body doesn't use insulin well. Most common form — about 90–95% of all diabetes cases. Can often be managed with lifestyle changes and medication.

Learn About Type 2 →
🤰

Gestational Diabetes

Develops in pregnant women who have never had diabetes. Usually resolves after birth but increases risk for type 2 diabetes later in life.

Learn More →
⚠️

Prediabetes

Blood sugar is higher than normal but not high enough for a diabetes diagnosis. Without action, prediabetes often progresses to type 2 diabetes.

Symptoms & Diagnosis

Many symptoms of diabetes can seem minor. Knowing what to look for can save lives.

Common symptoms include: frequent urination, increased thirst, unexplained weight loss, extreme fatigue, blurry vision, slow-healing cuts or bruises, tingling or numbness in hands or feet, and recurring infections. Some people with type 2 diabetes have no noticeable symptoms.

Diabetes is diagnosed through blood tests including: the Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG) test, the A1C test (measures average blood sugar over 2–3 months), the Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT), and the Random Plasma Glucose test. Your doctor will typically confirm a diagnosis with a second test on a different day.

Risk factors include being overweight or obese, being 45 years or older, having a parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes, being physically active fewer than 3 times per week, having had gestational diabetes, or being of African American, Hispanic/Latino, American Indian, or Alaska Native descent.

Unmanaged diabetes can lead to serious complications over time, including heart disease and stroke, kidney disease (nephropathy), eye damage (retinopathy) leading to blindness, nerve damage (neuropathy), foot problems that can lead to amputation, and dental disease. However, these complications can often be prevented or delayed with proper management.
