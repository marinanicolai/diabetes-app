Exercise & Blood Sugar

Regular physical activity is one of the most powerful tools available for managing diabetes. It helps your body use insulin more efficiently and lowers blood glucose levels during and after activity.

The ADA recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, spread over at least 3 days, with no more than 2 consecutive days without activity.

  • Walking, swimming, or cycling
  • Strength training 2–3 times per week
  • Stretching and flexibility exercises
  • Breaking up long periods of sitting every 30 minutes

Did You Know?

A single 30-minute walk can lower blood glucose by 20–50 mg/dL in people with type 2 diabetes.

Common Questions About Medications

Metformin is usually the first medication prescribed for type 2 diabetes. It works by reducing glucose production in the liver and improving insulin sensitivity. It's generally well-tolerated, inexpensive, and has a long safety record. Common side effects include nausea and digestive issues, especially when first starting.

GLP-1 receptor agonists (like semaglutide/Ozempic, liraglutide/Victoza) mimic a hormone that stimulates insulin secretion. They also slow digestion, reduce appetite, and support weight loss. Many also have proven cardiovascular and kidney-protective benefits.

Insulin may be needed for type 2 diabetes when blood sugar cannot be controlled with oral medications and lifestyle changes alone, during acute illness or surgery, during pregnancy, or when the pancreas progressively loses its ability to produce insulin. Starting insulin is not a sign of failure — it's a sign your treatment needs adjustment.

The A1C test measures your average blood glucose over the past 2–3 months. The ADA recommends an A1C target of below 7% for most adults with diabetes. Your personal target may be different — talk to your care team about what's right for you based on your age, health status, and risk of hypoglycemia.
